Sinkhorn on Gaussians: an exact convergence rate

A matrix recursion, a one-dimensional fraction, and what temperature really does to convergence.

Gabriel Peyré

Sinkhorn’s algorithm is wonderfully simple: alternately correct the two marginals of a coupling. Yet its speed can be surprisingly hard to predict. As the entropic temperature \(\varepsilon\) decreases, the transport plan becomes sharper and convergence usually slows down. How much slower: exponentially in \(1/\varepsilon\), or only proportionally to \(1/\varepsilon\)?

Gaussians offer a small laboratory in which this question can be answered exactly. For a quadratic cost, the entropic optimal coupling is Gaussian, and Sinkhorn stays within Gaussian distributions. This closed-form structure is studied in our work with Hicham Janati, Boris Muzellec, and Marco Cuturi [1], and in more detail by Akyildiz, Del Moral, and Miguez [2]. In one dimension, the entire iteration becomes a fractional-linear map.

The conclusion is encouraging: for centered Gaussian iterates, the number of full Sinkhorn cycles needed for a fixed relative reduction of a suitable error is of order \(1/\varepsilon\) at low temperature. A recent paper of Lénaïc Chizat, Alex Delalande, and Tomas Vaškevičius [3] explains why favorable temperature dependence extends well beyond Gaussians.

The problem and the convention

Let \(\alpha=\mathcal N(0,A)\) and \(\beta=\mathcal N(0,B)\), where \(A,B\) are positive-definite \(d\times d\) covariance matrices. We use the cost and regularized objective

\[ c(x,y)=\tfrac12|x-y|^2, \qquad J_\varepsilon(\pi)=\int c\,d\pi +\varepsilon\operatorname{KL}(\pi\mid\alpha\otimes\beta), \] \[ \min_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}J_\varepsilon(\pi). \]

Here \(\Pi(\alpha,\beta)\) is the set of joint laws with marginals \(\alpha,\beta\), and \(\operatorname{KL}(\pi\mid\eta)=\int\log(d\pi/d\eta)\,d\pi\), with value \(+\infty\) when absolute continuity fails. The factor \(1/2\) in the cost matters: changing it changes the temperature scale.

Sinkhorn alternates the two potential updates

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{k+1}(x)&=-\varepsilon\log\int e^{(g_k(y)-c(x,y))/\varepsilon}\,d\beta(y),\\ g_{k+1}(y)&=-\varepsilon\log\int e^{(f_{k+1}(x)-c(x,y))/\varepsilon}\,d\alpha(x). \end{aligned} \]

The associated density relative to \(\alpha\otimes\beta\) is \(e^{(f+g-c)/\varepsilon}\). The first update enforces the \(X\)-marginal; the second enforces the \(Y\)-marginal. Throughout this post, one cycle means both updates. After a full cycle, the second marginal is exactly \(\beta\), but the first generally is not yet \(\alpha\).

Gaussian closure: iterate conditional covariances

Quadratic potentials whose Gibbs density is integrable produce Gaussian laws, and integrating a Gaussian produces another quadratic potential. Starting with an admissible centered Gaussian initialization therefore preserves centered Gaussian joint laws. The target covariances \(A,B\) do not evolve; the useful evolving quantities are the conditional covariances

\[ S_k=\operatorname{Cov}(X\mid Y), \qquad R_k=\operatorname{Cov}(Y\mid X). \]

We observe the iteration just after each \(Y\)-normalization. It can be initialized at any \(S_0\succ0\) by taking \(Y\sim\beta\) and

\[ X\mid Y=y\ \sim\ \mathcal N(\varepsilon^{-1}S_0y,S_0). \]

The usual zero-potential initialization belongs to this family after its first full cycle. This indexing lets us state the recursion without a special first step.

Theorem 1 (the Gaussian Sinkhorn recursion). A full cycle is

\[ \begin{aligned} R_k&=(B^{-1}+\varepsilon^{-2}S_k)^{-1},\\ S_{k+1}&=(A^{-1}+\varepsilon^{-2}R_k)^{-1}. \end{aligned} \]

The joint covariance at the end of cycle \(k\) is

\[ \Sigma_k= \begin{pmatrix} S_k+\varepsilon^{-2}S_kBS_k & \varepsilon^{-1}S_kB\\ \varepsilon^{-1}BS_k & B \end{pmatrix}. \]

These iterates converge to the unique positive-definite solution of

\[ S_*+\varepsilon^{-2}S_*BS_*=A. \]

The limiting joint law therefore has both prescribed marginals and is the entropic optimizer. The proof below gives an explicit matrix-square-root formula for \(S_*\).

Proof: Gaussian conditioning and a matrix quadratic equation

The updates. A joint density in this family has the form

\[ p(x,y)\propto\exp\left( -\tfrac12x^\top P x+\varepsilon^{-1}x^\top y -\tfrac12y^\top Q y\right). \]

Here \(P,Q\) are diagonal blocks of the joint precision, not marginal precisions. Completing the square gives \(S=P^{-1}\), \(R=Q^{-1}\). To make the \(X\)-marginal have covariance \(A\), its Schur complement must be \(A^{-1}\): thus \(P_{\rm new}=A^{-1}+\varepsilon^{-2}Q^{-1}\). The following \(Y\)-normalization similarly gives \(Q_{\rm new}=B^{-1}+\varepsilon^{-2}P_{\rm new}^{-1}\). This proves the recursion. Finally, total covariance applied to \(X=\varepsilon^{-1}S_kY+Z\), with independent \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,S_k)\), gives \(\Sigma_k\).

The fixed point. Substituting the first update into the second shows that a fixed point satisfies \(S+\varepsilon^{-2}SBS=A\). Set

\[ D=\varepsilon^{-2}B^{1/2}SB^{1/2}, \qquad M=\varepsilon^{-2}B^{1/2}AB^{1/2}. \]

The equation becomes \(D+D^2=M\). Taking its unique positive-definite solution and undoing the change of variables gives the closed form

\[ D_*=\tfrac12(\sqrt{I+4M}-I), \] \[ S_*=\varepsilon^2 B^{-1/2}D_*B^{-1/2}. \]

No commutativity of \(A\) and \(B\) is assumed.

Why all positive-definite initializations converge

In the same variables, the iteration is

\[ D_{k+1}=\Phi(D_k),\qquad \Phi(D)=\big[M^{-1}+(I+D)^{-1}\big]^{-1}. \]

Matrix inversion reverses the positive-semidefinite order twice, so \(\Phi\) is order preserving, and \(0\prec\Phi(D)\prec M\) for \(D\succeq0\). The sequence started at \(0\) increases; the one started at \(M\) decreases. Both converge, and continuity forces both limits to solve \(D+D^2=M\). Uniqueness makes their limits equal to \(D_*\). For any positive-definite initialization, \(0\prec D_1\prec M\), hence

\[ \Phi^j(0)\preceq D_{j+1}\preceq\Phi^j(M) \quad(j\ge0). \]

The bounding sequences converge to the same limit, so \(D_k\to D_*\).

Optimality. The limiting density has the form \(d\pi_*/d(\alpha\otimes\beta)=e^{(f_*+g_*-c)/\varepsilon}\) and the required marginals. For any feasible \(\pi\) of finite objective, cancellation of the two potential integrals yields

\[ J_\varepsilon(\pi)-J_\varepsilon(\pi_*) =\varepsilon\operatorname{KL}(\pi\mid\pi_*)\ge0. \]

Equality forces \(\pi=\pi_*\). \(\square\)

One dimension: a fractional-linear iteration

Now let \(\alpha=\mathcal N(0,a)\), \(\beta=\mathcal N(0,b)\), where \(a,b>0\) are variances, not standard deviations. Write \(s_k>0\) for the conditional variance. The two inversions collapse to

\[ s_{k+1}=F(s_k),\qquad F(s)=\frac{a(bs+\varepsilon^2)}{bs+ab+\varepsilon^2}. \]

The current first marginal has variance \(\widetilde a_k=s_k+bs_k^2/\varepsilon^2\), and the cross-covariance is \(bs_k/\varepsilon\). Thus following a single positive number follows the entire coupling.

The two fixed points of the fractional-linear map are

\[ s_+=\frac{\varepsilon}{2b} \big(\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4ab}-\varepsilon\big), \qquad s_-=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2b} \big(\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4ab}+\varepsilon\big). \]

Only \(s_*=s_+\) is a variance. The negative root is nevertheless useful: measuring the distance to one fixed point relative to the other linearizes the dynamics.

Theorem 2 (an exact scalar convergence law). Define the optimal correlation and the full-cycle factor

\[ \rho=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4ab}-\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{ab}}, \qquad q=\rho^4\in(0,1). \]

For every \(s_0>0\), the cross-ratio error satisfies

\[ z_k:=\frac{s_k-s_*}{s_k-s_-}=q^kz_0. \]

Consequently the entire trajectory is explicit:

\[ s_k=\frac{s_*-q^kz_0s_-}{1-q^kz_0}. \]

Unless \(s_0=s_*\), the asymptotic ratio of conditional-variance errors is

\[ \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{|s_{k+1}-s_*|}{|s_k-s_*|}=q. \]
Proof: move the two fixed points to zero and infinity

A fractional-linear map fixing \(s_*\) and \(s_-\) becomes a multiplication in the coordinate \(z=(s-s_*)/(s-s_-)\). Here direct substitution gives

\[ \frac{F(s)-s_*}{F(s)-s_-} =\left( \frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4ab}-\varepsilon} {\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4ab}+\varepsilon} \right)^2\frac{s-s_*}{s-s_-}. \]

The squared prefactor is \(\rho^4\), since the product of the two terms \(\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4ab}\pm\varepsilon\) is \(4ab\). Iteration and solving for \(s_k\) prove the formulas. For \(s_0>0\), \(-\rho^2<z_0<1\), so the denominator never vanishes. Finally,

\[ s_k-s_*=(s_*-s_-)\frac{q^kz_0}{1-q^kz_0}, \]

which gives the limiting error ratio. \(\square\)

The same asymptotic factor applies to the cross-covariance error and to the first-marginal variance error: the former is linear in \(s_k\), and the latter has a nonzero derivative at \(s_*\).

What happens at low temperature?

The natural dimensionless temperature is \(\tau=\varepsilon/\sqrt{ab}\). Indeed, the optimal joint covariance is

\[ \Sigma_*= \begin{pmatrix}a&\sqrt{ab}\,\rho\\\sqrt{ab}\,\rho&b\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho=e^{-\operatorname{arsinh}(\tau/2)}. \]

Low temperature makes \(\rho\) close to one: the coupling concentrates near the deterministic map \(Y=\sqrt{b/a}\,X\). The two marginal corrections then undo much of each other’s progress. Quantitatively,

\[ -\log q=4\operatorname{arsinh}(\tau/2), \qquad q=1-2\tau+O(\tau^2). \]

To reduce \(|z_k|\) by a factor \(\eta\in(0,1)\), a nonstationary initialization therefore needs exactly

\[ k_\eta= \left\lceil\frac{\log(1/\eta)}{4\operatorname{arsinh}(\tau/2)}\right\rceil \sim\frac{\sqrt{ab}}{2\varepsilon}\log\frac1\eta \quad(\varepsilon\to0). \]

This is a statement about relative reduction of the cross-ratio error. Other errors have the same asymptotic geometric factor, but their initial sizes and the requested absolute tolerance also affect iteration counts. At high temperature, \(q\sim\tau^{-4}\), so only a few cycles are needed.

Which error, and which iterates? The factor \(q=\rho^4\) concerns centered Gaussian covariance errors per full cycle. The normalized dual objective gap is locally quadratic in this error, so its asymptotic factor is \(q^2=\rho^8=1-4\tau+O(\tau^2)\). Arbitrary noncentered Gaussian initializations can have slower mean modes, with factor \(\rho^2\). Centered marginals alone do not remove such modes; our centered quadratic initialization does.

Why the dual objective gap has the squared rate

For a normalized Gibbs coupling \(\pi_k\), define the dual value

\[ \mathcal D(f,g)=\int f\,d\alpha+\int g\,d\beta -\varepsilon\int\big(e^{(f+g-c)/\varepsilon}-1\big) \,d\alpha\,d\beta. \]

Since \(\pi_k\) has total mass one, the last integral is zero. Using the marginals of \(\pi_*\) in the log-density ratio gives the exact identity

\[ \delta_k:=\mathcal D(f_*,g_*)-\mathcal D(f_k,g_k) =\varepsilon\operatorname{KL}(\pi_*\mid\pi_k). \]

Both couplings have the same \(Y\)-marginal. Integrating the conditional Gaussian relative entropy, and putting \(u=s_k/s_*\), gives

\[ \frac{\delta_k}{\varepsilon} =\frac12\left[ \log u+\frac1u-1 +\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho^2}\frac{(u-1)^2}{u}\right]. \]

This is a positive constant times \((u-1)^2\), to leading order near \(1\). Thus \(\delta_{k+1}/\delta_k\to q^2\) for a nonstationary trajectory. Notice that \(\pi_k\) generally has only one correct marginal: plugging it into the primal objective would not give a feasible primal optimality gap.

For comparison, if the potentials contain linear terms with coefficients \(\ell,m\), Gaussian integration gives \(\ell_{\rm new}=-R m/\varepsilon\), then \(m_{\rm new}=-S_{\rm new}\ell_{\rm new}/\varepsilon\). At the scalar covariance fixed point their full-cycle multiplier is \(S_*R_*/\varepsilon^2=\rho^2\). Our centered initialization sets these coefficients to zero throughout.

Why a worst-case bound looks much worse

Classical Sinkhorn analysis uses the Hilbert projective metric [4], [5]. For a bounded cost with oscillation \(C=\sup c-\inf c\), the kernel \(K=e^{-c/\varepsilon}\) has projective diameter at most \(2C/\varepsilon\). Birkhoff’s theorem then gives the full-cycle contraction bound

\[ \overline q_{\mathrm H}=\tanh^2\!\left(\frac{C}{2\varepsilon}\right), \qquad 1-\overline q_{\mathrm H}\sim4e^{-C/\varepsilon}. \]

The exponentially small quantity is the improvement \(1-\overline q_{\mathrm H}\), not the contraction factor itself: that factor approaches one. This elementary guarantee can lead to a cycle count of order \(e^{C/\varepsilon}\log(1/\eta)\). For half-squared distance on a common ball of radius \(R\), one can take \(C\le2R^2\).

This is a worst-case bound, not a prediction that every instance is exponentially slow. It uses extreme kernel ratios and ignores the geometry of the marginals. Moreover, Gaussian supports are unbounded, so the bounded-cost estimate is actually vacuous for them. The exact calculation above is a different, structure-sensitive analysis: its decay exponent is proportional to \(\varepsilon\), not exponentially small in \(1/\varepsilon\).

Beyond Gaussians

Replacing \(\tfrac12|x-y|^2\) by \(-\langle x,y\rangle\) subtracts fixed marginal integrals, so it preserves the optimizer and the temperature. For this bilinear cost, Chizat, Delalande, and Vaškevičius [3, Theorems 1.1–1.2] prove substantially sharper bounds. With compact supports, a convex source domain, and a log-concave source density, their dual-gap bound has a full-cycle factor of the form \(1-c_0\varepsilon\) at low temperature, where \(c_0>0\) depends on the fixed problem geometry. The target marginal need not have a density.

For a source density bounded above and below by positive constants on a compact convex domain, they obtain a global \(1-c_1\varepsilon^2\) factor, improving eventually to \(1-c_2\varepsilon\). These are structural assumptions, not a claim that smoothness alone always suffices. Their Gaussian example separately establishes the sharp linear dependence on temperature for the asymptotic dual-gap rate; it is not an application of their compact-support theorem.

The Gaussian calculation thus captures a useful lesson: the notorious low-temperature slowdown is real, but its severity depends on more than the smallest entry of the Gibbs kernel. Once the distributional structure enters the argument, a much more informative rate becomes visible.
