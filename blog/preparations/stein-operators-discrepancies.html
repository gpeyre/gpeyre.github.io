Stein operators and probability discrepancies

Characterizing distributions by integration by parts, and measuring the failure of these identities.

Gabriel Peyré

For a standard Gaussian random variable \(Z\), integration by parts gives \(\mathbb E[f'(Z)]=\mathbb E[Zf(Z)]\). This is more than a formula for computing expectations: if a probability distribution satisfies it for every suitable test function, that distribution must be Gaussian.

Charles Stein used this characterization to study normal approximation [1]. Here we prove the identity and its converse, then extend them to smooth densities in several dimensions. Taking the largest violation over a unit ball of test functions gives a Stein discrepancy. Bounded-Lipschitz tests provide bounds on transport distances. Other choices of norm recover relative Fisher information or give a discrepancy computable from samples.

Gaussian integration by parts

Write \(\gamma(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/2}\) for the standard Gaussian density. Since \(\gamma'(x)=-x\gamma(x)\), define the one-dimensional Gaussian Stein operator by

\[ \mathcal T_\gamma f(x)=f'(x)-xf(x) =\frac{(\gamma f)'(x)}{\gamma(x)}. \]

For \(f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)\), meaning a smooth function with compact support,

\[ \mathbb E_\gamma[\mathcal T_\gamma f] =\int_{\mathbb R}(\gamma f)'(x)\,dx=0. \]

The same calculation applies to noncompactly supported functions when the terms are integrable and the boundary term vanishes. In particular it applies to polynomials under a Gaussian. Choosing \(f(x)=1,x,x^2,x^3\) gives, successively, its first four moment identities. For a general candidate law, the first two tests only check its mean and second moment; they do not characterize its shape.

For \(Z\sim\mathcal N(m,\sigma^2)\), with \(\sigma>0\), the corresponding identity is

\[ \mathbb E[(Z-m)f(Z)]=\sigma^2\mathbb E[f'(Z)]. \]

This follows either by rescaling or by differentiating the Gaussian density. Multiplying a Stein operator by a nonzero constant does not change its zero-expectation identities.

Numerical experiment: Gaussian-mixture tests

The dashed reference is always \(P=\mathcal N(0,1)\). Two sliders change the candidate distribution: the first separates and narrows three Gaussian components, while the second moves them together along the real line. Neither slider represents time. For \(\theta,b\in[-1,1]\), put

\[ a=2\theta,\qquad \sigma=1-0.75|\theta|, \qquad m=1.5b, \] \[ \begin{aligned} Q_{\theta,b}={}&0.2\,\mathcal N(m-1.1a,\sigma^2)\\ &+0.5\,\mathcal N(m-0.1a,\sigma^2) +0.3\,\mathcal N(m+0.9a,\sigma^2). \end{aligned} \]

At \(\theta=0\), the components coincide and give a single Gaussian. Setting both sliders to zero recovers the standard Gaussian exactly. As \(|\theta|\) increases, the bumps separate and their standard deviation decreases from \(1\) to \(1/4\); changing its sign reflects their relative arrangement. The position slider translates all three bumps without changing their weights or widths. In this family, the mean is \(m\) and the variance is \(\sigma^2+0.49a^2\): neither is constrained to match the reference.

\[ R_f(\theta,b)=\mathbb E_{Q_{\theta,b}}[f'(X)]- \mathbb E_{Q_{\theta,b}}[Xf(X)]. \]

Zero is at the vertical center: positive residuals extend upward, negative ones downward. A small sketch of \(f\) appears below each bar. The next two, \(f=x^2-1\) and \(f=x^3-3x\), give \(3\mathbb E X-\mathbb E X^3\) and \(6\mathbb E X^2-\mathbb E X^4-3\). They probe more than the mean and variance. A finite panel of tests is not a certificate that an arbitrary distribution is Gaussian; the following characterization requires an entire function class.

Characterization of the Gaussian law

Theorem 1. A probability measure \(Q\) on \(\mathbb R\) is the standard Gaussian law if and only if

\[ \int\mathcal T_\gamma f\,dQ=0 \qquad\text{for every }f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R). \]

No moment assumption on \(Q\) is needed in this statement: both \(f'\) and \(xf\) are bounded and compactly supported. The condition is an infinite family of identities, not a finite list of moment constraints.

Proof: solve the first-order equation for centered tests

The forward implication was just proved. For the converse, first take a smooth, compactly supported function \(g\) with \(\int g\gamma=0\). The function

\[ f(x)=\frac1{\gamma(x)}\int_{-\infty}^x g(t)\gamma(t)\,dt \]

is smooth and compactly supported: the integral vanishes before the support of \(g\), and also after it because its total integral is zero. Differentiation gives \(\mathcal T_\gamma f=g\), so the assumed identities imply \(\int g\,dQ=0\).

To handle an arbitrary \(h\in C_c^\infty\), fix a nonnegative bump \(\eta\in C_c^\infty\) with \(\int\eta\gamma=1\), and apply the previous argument to \(g=h-(\int h\gamma)\eta\). We obtain

\[ \int h\,dQ=c\int h\gamma, \qquad c=\int\eta\,dQ. \]

Equality against all smooth compactly supported tests identifies the measures: \(Q=c\gamma(x)\,dx\). Since both have mass one, \(c=1\). \(\square\)

The equation used in this proof is also the starting point of Stein's method. For a bounded smooth observable \(h\), solve

\[ \mathcal T_\gamma f_h=h-\mathbb E_\gamma h, \qquad f_h(x)=\frac1{\gamma(x)}\int_{-\infty}^x (h(t)-\mathbb E_\gamma h)\gamma(t)\,dt. \]

Whenever the expectations exist,

\[ \mathbb E_Q h-\mathbb E_\gamma h =\mathbb E_Q[\mathcal T_\gamma f_h]. \]

Thus an error in distribution becomes an expectation involving a derivative. Bounds on the solutions \(f_h\), uniform over a chosen class of observables \(h\), turn this identity into normal-approximation bounds. This is the link with questions about rates in the central limit theorem.

Smooth densities and the divergence-form operator

Let \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d\) be a connected open set, and let \(p\) be a strictly positive \(C^1\) probability density on \(\Omega\). Its score is the vector field

\[ s_p(x)=\nabla\log p(x). \]

For a vector-valued test function \(f:\Omega\to\mathbb R^d\), define the Langevin Stein operator

\[ \boxed{\quad \mathcal T_p f =\operatorname{div}f+s_p\cdot f =\frac1p\operatorname{div}(pf). \quad} \]

The divergence is \(\operatorname{div}f=\sum_{j=1}^d\partial_j f_j\), so the output is a scalar. For every \(f\in C_c^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^d)\), integration of the divergence gives \(\mathbb E_p[\mathcal T_p f]=0\). The normalizing constant cancels: if \(p=e^{-V}/Z\), then \(s_p=-\nabla V\), whether or not \(Z\) can be computed.

Theorem 2. Under these assumptions, a probability measure \(Q\) on \(\Omega\) equals \(p(x)\,dx\) if and only if \(\mathbb E_Q[\mathcal T_p f]=0\) for every \(f\in C_c^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^d)\).

Proof: the identities determine the density up to a constant

First suppose \(Q\) has a positive \(C^1\) density \(q\). Integration by parts against \(q\) gives

\[ \mathbb E_q[\mathcal T_p f] =\int q\,(s_p-s_q)\cdot f =-\int q\,\nabla\log(q/p)\cdot f. \]

If this is zero for every compactly supported field, then \(\nabla\log(q/p)=0\). Connectedness implies \(q=cp\), and normalization gives \(c=1\).

The density assumption on \(Q\) is unnecessary. Define the locally finite measure \(d\nu=p^{-1}dQ\). Testing with \(f=g/p\) for \(g\in C_c^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^d)\) yields

\[ \int\operatorname{div}g\,d\nu=0. \]

All distributional derivatives of \(\nu\) therefore vanish. Such a distribution is constant on a connected open set: locally convolve it with a smooth compactly supported mollifier; the result has zero gradient and is constant on each ball, and overlapping balls identify the constants. Passing to the limit gives \(\nu=c\,dx\). Thus \(Q=cp\,dx\), and normalization finishes the proof. \(\square\)

The assumptions have a purpose. On a smooth bounded domain, test fields extending to the boundary produce the boundary term \(\int_{\partial\Omega}pf\cdot n\); it must vanish. On a disconnected support, local tests cannot determine how much probability lies in each component. For a singular or discrete target, \(\nabla\log p\) is not available in this form; other Stein operators are needed. For instance, the Poisson law with mean \(\lambda\) satisfies \(\mathbb E[\lambda f(N+1)-Nf(N)]=0\) for bounded \(f\), by shifting the summation index. The extension is to general distributions through suitable operators, not through a density formula that applies to every measure [2].

For \(P=\mathcal N(m,\Sigma)\), with \(\Sigma\) positive definite, the formula specializes to

\[ \mathcal T_p f(x)=\operatorname{div}f(x) -\langle\Sigma^{-1}(x-m),f(x)\rangle. \]

Equivalently, scalar tests \(u\) satisfy \(\mathbb E[(X-m)u(X)]=\Sigma\mathbb E[\nabla u(X)]\). This is the multivariate Gaussian integration-by-parts identity.

The Langevin generator

The first-order Stein operator acts on a vector field. Restricting it to gradient fields \(f=\nabla u\) gives a second-order operator on scalar functions:

\[ \mathcal L_pu=\mathcal T_p(\nabla u) =\Delta u+s_p\cdot\nabla u =\frac1p\operatorname{div}(p\nabla u). \]

On \(\mathbb R^d\), with sufficient regularity and growth control for the diffusion to be well posed, this is the generator of

\[ dX_t=s_p(X_t)\,dt+\sqrt2\,dB_t. \]

Here \(B_t\) is standard Brownian motion; the factor \(\sqrt2\) fixes the convention for the Laplacian. If \(q_t\) denotes the evolving density, its Fokker–Planck equation is

\[ \partial_tq_t=\Delta q_t-\operatorname{div}(q_ts_p) =\operatorname{div}\!\left(q_t\nabla\log\frac{q_t}{p}\right). \]

Substituting \(q_t=p\) gives zero. Under the usual integrability and decay assumptions, \(p\) is invariant. For the standard Gaussian, this is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck diffusion \(dX_t=-X_t\,dt+\sqrt2\,dB_t\). The distinction between \(\mathcal T_p\) and \(\mathcal L_p\) avoids confusing a vector-field identity with a scalar diffusion generator [3].

From identities to discrepancies

Fix a class \(\mathcal F\) of test fields on which the Stein identity holds under \(P\), and for which the following expectations under \(Q\) exist. Define

\[ \mathcal S_{\mathcal F}(Q,P) =\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left|\mathbb E_Q[\mathcal T_p f]\right|. \]

Usually \(\mathcal F\) is a unit ball in a normed function space. The norm prevents a nonzero residual from being made arbitrarily large just by multiplying its test field. If the ball is symmetric, the absolute value can be omitted.

This quantity vanishes when \(Q=P\). Whether the converse holds depends on the test class: Theorem 2 uses all compactly supported fields, whereas a few polynomial tests miss many distributions. A Stein discrepancy is generally asymmetric, and is not automatically a distance or a divergence separating every pair of probability measures. The operator alone does not specify the discrepancy.

A Wasserstein bound

Let \(Q\) be a probability measure on \(\mathbb R\) with finite first moment. Its Wasserstein distance to the standard Gaussian is the least average distance needed to transport one law to the other:

\[ W_1(Q,\gamma) =\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(Q,\gamma)}\int_{\mathbb R^2}|x-y|\,d\pi(x,y). \]

Here \(\Pi(Q,\gamma)\) consists of joint laws with marginals \(Q\) and \(\gamma\). Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality expresses the same distance using observables:

\[ W_1(Q,\gamma) =\sup_{\operatorname{Lip}(h)\le1} |\mathbb E_Qh-\mathbb E_\gamma h|. \]

There is no bound on the amplitude of \(h\). The Stein equation converts these Lipschitz observables into tests for the discrepancy

\[ S_\gamma(Q) =\sup_{\substack{f\in C^1(\mathbb R)\\ \|f\|_\infty\le1,\;\|f'\|_\infty\le1}} \left|\mathbb E_Q[f'(X)-Xf(X)]\right|. \]

The first-moment assumption makes these expectations finite, since \(|f'(x)-xf(x)|\le1+|x|\). Gaussian integration by parts gives \(S_\gamma(\gamma)=0\).

Theorem 3 (Wasserstein bound). For every probability measure \(Q\) with finite first moment,

\[ \boxed{\quad W_1(Q,\gamma)\le S_\gamma(Q). \quad} \]
Proof using the Gaussian Stein equation

Take any 1-Lipschitz \(h\), possibly unbounded. Subtracting \(h(0)\) changes neither the difference of expectations nor the centered Stein equation, so we may assume \(h(0)=0\). Then \(|h(x)|\le|x|\), and both expectations exist. Write \(\gamma(h)=\mathbb E_\gamma h\). The function

\[ f_h(x)=\frac1{\gamma(x)}\int_{-\infty}^x (h(t)-\gamma(h))\gamma(t)\,dt \]

belongs to \(C^1\) and solves \(\mathcal T_\gamma f_h=h-\gamma(h)\). The Gaussian Stein-factor bounds apply to all Lipschitz observables, including unbounded ones [8]:

\[ \begin{aligned} \|f_h\|_\infty&\le\operatorname{Lip}(h)\le1,\\ \|f_h'\|_\infty&\le\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\operatorname{Lip}(h)\le1. \end{aligned} \]

These estimates depend on the Lipschitz constant, not on \(\|h\|_\infty\). Thus \(f_h\) is admissible in \(S_\gamma\). Integrating its equation against the probability measure \(Q\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} |\mathbb E_Qh-\mathbb E_\gamma h| &=\left|\mathbb E_Q[\mathcal T_\gamma f_h]\right|\\ &\le S_\gamma(Q). \end{aligned} \]

Take the supremum over all 1-Lipschitz \(h\), and apply Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality. \(\square\)

The observable \(h\) and the Stein test function \(f\) play different roles: \(h\) is merely 1-Lipschitz, while both \(f\) and \(f'\) are bounded. The result is an upper bound, not an equality. For example, \(S_\gamma(\delta_0)=1\), attained by \(f(x)=\tanh x\), whereas \(W_1(\delta_0,\gamma)=\mathbb E_\gamma|X|=\sqrt{2/\pi}\).

An L² unit ball: relative Fisher information

Suppose now that \(q,p\) are positive \(C^1\) densities on the same connected domain. Take the candidate-dependent unit ball

\[ \mathcal F_q= \left\{f\in C_c^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^d): \int\|f\|^2q\le1\right\}. \]

Proposition 1. If the relative Fisher information is finite, then

\[ \mathcal S_{\mathcal F_q}(Q,P)^2=I(Q\mid P), \qquad I(Q\mid P)=\int\left\|\nabla\log\frac qp\right\|^2q. \]
Proof by L² duality

The integration-by-parts formula in Theorem 2 reads \(\mathbb E_q[\mathcal T_pf]=\langle s_p-s_q,f\rangle_{L^2(q)}\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives the upper bound \(\sqrt{I(Q\mid P)}\). For the reverse inequality, approximate \((s_p-s_q)/\sqrt I\) in \(L^2(q)\) by compactly supported \(C^1\) fields and normalize them into the unit ball. Such fields are dense in \(L^2(q)\). Their pairings tend to \(\sqrt I\). The case \(I=0\) is immediate. \(\square\)

Thus Fisher information is the square of this Stein discrepancy, not the discrepancy itself. It measures the mismatch of the two scores, averaged under \(q\). For example,

\[ Q=\mathcal N(m,\sigma^2),\quad P=\mathcal N(0,1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I(Q\mid P)=m^2+(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})^2. \]

To check the formula, write \(X=m+\sigma Z\): then \(s_q(X)-s_p(X)=m+(\sigma-\sigma^{-1})Z\). More generally, using an \(L^r(q)\) unit ball, with \(1\lt r\lt\infty\), gives the \(L^{r'}(q)\) norm of the score difference, where \(1/r+1/r'=1\). These are score-based discrepancies, not generally Csiszár \(f\)-divergences.

This also explains score matching [4]. Under sufficient regularity, integrability of the displayed terms, and vanishing boundary terms, expanding the square gives

\[ \tfrac12 I(Q\mid P) =\mathbb E_q\!\left[\tfrac12\|s_p\|^2+ \operatorname{div}s_p\right] +\tfrac12\mathbb E_q\|s_q\|^2. \]

The last term does not depend on the model \(p\). Thus one can fit its score using expectations under the data distribution without evaluating the data score \(s_q\) or the model's normalizing constant. This objective is not the full Fisher information for an empirical measure, which has no smooth positive density.

An RKHS unit ball: a sample-based discrepancy

For a discrepancy that can be evaluated directly on samples, take a smooth positive-definite kernel \(k(x,y)\) and its reproducing kernel Hilbert space \(\mathcal H_k\). For vector fields use the product space \(\mathcal H_k^d\), with squared norm equal to the sum of the component squared norms. Assume its fields satisfy the Stein identity for \(p\), derivatives reproduce in the RKHS, and the feature expectations below are integrable. The unit ball gives the kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD), developed for goodness-of-fit testing in [5] and [6].

Proposition 2. With \(X,Y\) independent and distributed according to \(Q\),

\[ \operatorname{KSD}_k(Q,P)^2 =\mathbb E[k_p(X,Y)], \] \[ \begin{aligned} k_p(x,y)={}&k(x,y)s_p(x)\cdot s_p(y)\\ &+s_p(x)\cdot\nabla_y k(x,y) +s_p(y)\cdot\nabla_x k(x,y)\\ &+\operatorname{tr}(\nabla_x\nabla_y k(x,y)). \end{aligned} \]
Proof using the reproducing property

Derivative reproduction gives the feature field

\[ \xi_p(x)=s_p(x)k(x,\cdot)+\nabla_x k(x,\cdot) \in\mathcal H_k^d, \]

such that \(\mathcal T_p f(x)=\langle f,\xi_p(x)\rangle_{\mathcal H_k^d}\). Consequently the supremum over the unit ball is \(\|w_Q\|_{\mathcal H_k^d}\), where \(w_Q=\mathbb E_Q\xi_p(X)\). Squaring, taking two independent copies, and expanding the inner product gives the formula, since \(k_p(x,y)=\langle\xi_p(x),\xi_p(y)\rangle\). A sufficient integrability condition is \(\mathbb E_Q\|\xi_p(X)\|<\infty\). \(\square\)

For example, the Gaussian kernel \(k(x,y)=\exp(-\|x-y\|^2/(2h^2))\), with bandwidth \(h>0\), has explicit derivatives. Its Stein kernel is

\[ k_p(x,y)=k(x,y)\left[ s_p(x)\cdot s_p(y) +\frac{(s_p(x)-s_p(y))\cdot(x-y)}{h^2} +\frac d{h^2}-\frac{\|x-y\|^2}{h^4}\right]. \]

For the empirical measure \(Q_n=n^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{x_i}\), the squared KSD is exactly

\[ \operatorname{KSD}_k(Q_n,P)^2 =\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j=1}^n k_p(x_i,x_j). \]

It requires only the sample locations, the target score, and kernel derivatives. The diagonal terms are included, and the result is nonnegative. The off-diagonal unbiased estimator used in statistical testing is a different quantity and can be negative.

A caution: not every kernel gives a separating discrepancy, and even separation does not imply that a vanishing discrepancy forces weak convergence. Gorham and Mackey give counterexamples for commonly used kernels and conditions under which other kernels do control convergence [7]. The test space is a mathematical choice, not just a plotting parameter.

Relative entropy and Langevin evolution

The Fisher example has a useful interpretation in optimal transport. For smooth positive densities on \(\mathbb R^d\) with finite \(\operatorname{KL}(q\mid p)\), let \(q_t=(\mathrm{Id}+tf)_\#q\) for a smooth compactly supported vector field \(f\). Differentiating relative entropy at \(t=0\) gives

\[ \left.\frac d{dt}\operatorname{KL}(q_t\mid p)\right|_{t=0} =-\mathbb E_q[\mathcal T_p f]. \]
Proof by change of variables

For sufficiently small \(t\), \(F_t(x)=x+tf(x)\) is a diffeomorphism. The change-of-variables formula yields

\[ \operatorname{KL}((F_t)_\#q\mid p) =\int q(x)\left[ \log q(x)-\log\det(I+tDf(x))-\log p(F_t(x)) \right]dx. \]

Assuming the entropy is finite, differentiation of the varying terms is justified by compact support and smoothness. Their derivatives at zero are \(-\operatorname{div}f\) and \(-s_p\cdot f\). \(\square\)

The largest instantaneous entropy decrease among fields of \(L^2(q)\) norm at most one is therefore \(\sqrt{I(Q\mid P)}\). Along the Langevin Fokker–Planck equation, integration by parts gives the corresponding dissipation identity

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac d{dt}\operatorname{KL}(q_t\mid p) &=\int\log(q_t/p)\, \operatorname{div}\!\left(q_t\nabla\log(q_t/p)\right)\\ &=-I(Q_t\mid P). \end{aligned} \]

Here smoothness, integrability, and decay are needed to justify differentiation and remove boundary terms. This is the same quantity that appears in logarithmic Sobolev inequalities and convergence of Langevin diffusion. A small Fisher information measures a small entropy gradient; converting it into an entropy or distance bound needs an additional inequality.
