From Classical to Quantum Pinsker Inequality

One sign set, one matrix sign, and the same sharp constant.

Gabriel Peyré

Relative entropy is asymmetric, can be infinite, and is not a distance. Nevertheless, a small relative entropy forces two probability distributions to be close in a very concrete sense: no event can have very different probabilities under the two laws. This is the content of Pinsker’s inequality.

The same statement survives when probabilities become density matrices, even if the matrices do not commute. The proof below keeps the essential idea visible. In the classical case, collect all positive discrepancies into one event. In the quantum case, collect the positive eigenvalues of the discrepancy into one observable. A weighted Cauchy–Schwarz inequality then does the work.

All logarithms are natural, and all matrices are finite-dimensional. The classical and quantum inequalities are standard; see Watrous [1] and Wirth [2]. The purpose here is a direct matrix-calculus proof, without assuming the quantum data-processing theorem.

First, two classical outcomes are enough

For probability vectors \(a,b\), define the relative entropy and the full \(\ell^1\) discrepancy by

\[ D(a\Vert b)=\sum_i a_i\log\frac{a_i}{b_i},\qquad \lVert a-b\rVert_1=\sum_i|a_i-b_i|. \]

Use \(0\log(0/b)=0\), and set \(D=+\infty\) if some \(a_i>0=b_i\). The total variation distance is half the full norm: \(\operatorname{TV}(a,b)=\lVert a-b\rVert_1/2\).

Theorem 1 (classical Pinsker). For any probability vectors,

\[ D(a\Vert b)\ge\frac12\lVert a-b\rVert_1^2 =2\operatorname{TV}(a,b)^2. \]
Proof: merge the positive and negative discrepancies

Let \(A=\{i:a_i>b_i\}\), \(p=\sum_{i\in A}a_i\), and \(q=\sum_{i\in A}b_i\). Since the total signed discrepancy is zero,

\[ p-q=\frac12\lVert a-b\rVert_1. \]

Merging entries in \(A\) and in its complement cannot increase relative entropy:

\[ D(a\Vert b)\ge d(p\mid q),\qquad d(p\mid q)=p\log\frac pq+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}. \]
Lemma 1: the log-sum inequality behind this merging

For \(u_i,v_i>0\), write \(U=\sum_i u_i\), \(V=\sum_i v_i\), and \(\phi(s)=s\log s\). Jensen’s inequality gives

\[ \sum_i u_i\log\frac{u_i}{v_i} =V\sum_i\frac{v_i}{V}\phi\!\left(\frac{u_i}{v_i}\right) \ge V\phi(U/V)=U\log(U/V). \]

Limits cover zero entries, with the stated infinite-value convention. Apply this once on each sign region. \(\square\)

For fixed \(0<q<1\), the function \(p\mapsto d(p\mid q)\) vanishes to first order at \(p=q\), and

\[ \partial_p^2d(p\mid q)=\frac1{p(1-p)}\ge4. \]

Integrating this curvature bound twice yields \(d(p\mid q)\ge2(p-q)^2\), proving the theorem. The boundary cases follow by limits or have infinite entropy. \(\square\)

The proof also works for probability measures. Take densities with respect to their sum and replace finite sums by integrals. The sign set remains the event that detects the full total variation discrepancy.

What replaces the sign set for matrices?

A density matrix is a positive semidefinite Hermitian matrix of trace one. Its eigenvalues are probabilities, but its eigenvectors also matter. For two such matrices, the Umegaki relative entropy [3] and the trace norm are

\[ D(\rho\Vert\sigma)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\bigr), \qquad \lVert X\rVert_1=\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{X^*X}. \]

The formula is interpreted on the support of \(\sigma\). If \(\operatorname{supp}\rho\not\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\sigma\), set \(D=+\infty\); use \(0\log0=0\) for zero eigenvalues. Commuting density matrices reduce to classical probability vectors in a common eigenbasis.

Put \(\Delta=\rho-\sigma\), and let \(\Pi\) be the spectral projector onto the strictly positive eigenspace of \(\Delta\). The observable

\[ H=2\Pi-I \quad\text{satisfies}\quad H^2=I,\qquad \operatorname{tr}(\Delta H)=\lVert\Delta\rVert_1. \]

It assigns \(+1\) to positive discrepancies and \(−1\) to the remaining eigenspaces; zero discrepancies do not affect the trace. This is the matrix counterpart of choosing \(A\). The two-outcome measurement \((\Pi,I-\Pi)\) attains the full trace-distance discrepancy, a basic fact in quantum state discrimination [1].

Theorem 2 (quantum Pinsker). For any density matrices,

\[ D(\rho\Vert\sigma)\ge\frac12\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_1^2. \]
Proof: entropy curvature tested against the sign observable

First assume \(\rho,\sigma>0\). Interpolate linearly:

\[ M_t=\sigma+t\Delta,\qquad F(t)=D(M_t\Vert\sigma),\qquad 0\le t\le1. \]

We have \(F(0)=F'(0)=0\). It suffices to show \(F''(t)\ge\lVert\Delta\rVert_1^2\).

Step 1: compute the curvature in the current eigenbasis. Write \(M_t=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)\) in an orthonormal eigenbasis. Then

\[ F''(t)=\sum_{i,j}L_{ij}|\Delta_{ij}|^2, \qquad L_{ij}=\int_0^1\frac{du}{(1-u)\lambda_i+u\lambda_j} \ge\frac{2}{\lambda_i+\lambda_j}. \]

The last bound is scalar Jensen for \(s\mapsto1/s\). The divided difference is \(L_{ij}=(\log\lambda_i-\log\lambda_j)/(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\), with value \(1/\lambda_i\) when the eigenvalues coincide.

Lemma 2: differentiating the matrix logarithm, without differentiating eigenvectors

The scalar resolvent integral, applied by the spectral theorem, gives

\[ \log M=\int_0^\infty\left(\frac{I}{1+s}-(M+sI)^{-1}\right)ds. \]

Differentiate using \(\mathrm D(A^{-1})[E]=-A^{-1}EA^{-1}\):

\[ \mathrm D\log_M[E] =\int_0^\infty(M+sI)^{-1}E(M+sI)^{-1}ds. \]

Positive definiteness bounds the differentiated integrand near zero and makes it integrable at infinity. In the eigenbasis of \(M\), the \((i,j)\) entry is \(L_{ij}E_{ij}\). The diagonal entries also show \(\operatorname{tr}(M\,\mathrm D\log_M[E])=\operatorname{tr}E\). Thus differentiating \(\operatorname{tr}(M\log M)\) gives \(\operatorname{tr}(E(\log M+I))\). Since \(\operatorname{tr}\Delta=0\),

\[ F'(t)=\operatorname{tr}(\Delta(\log M_t-\log\sigma)), \qquad F''(t)=\operatorname{tr}(\Delta\,\mathrm D\log_{M_t}[\Delta]), \]

which is the claimed formula. \(\square\)

Step 2: insert the sign observable. Weighted Cauchy–Schwarz, in the same eigenbasis, gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \lVert\Delta\rVert_1^2 &=\left|\sum_{i,j}\Delta_{ij}\overline{H_{ij}}\right|^2\\ &\le\left(\sum_{i,j}\frac{2|\Delta_{ij}|^2}{\lambda_i+\lambda_j}\right) \left(\sum_{i,j}\frac{\lambda_i+\lambda_j}{2}|H_{ij}|^2\right)\\ &\le F''(t)\operatorname{tr}(M_tH^2)=F''(t). \end{aligned} \]

The second factor equals \(\operatorname{tr}(M_tH^2)=1\): Hermitian symmetry equates the row and column sums, \(H^2=I\), and \(\operatorname{tr}M_t=1\). No commutation between \(H\) and \(M_t\) is required.

Step 3: integrate twice. Therefore

\[ D(\rho\Vert\sigma)=\int_0^1(1-t)F''(t)dt \ge\frac12\lVert\Delta\rVert_1^2. \]

Singular states. If the support condition fails, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise restrict everything to \(\operatorname{supp}\sigma\), where \(\sigma>0\). Apply the result to \(\rho_\eta=(1-\eta)\rho+\eta\sigma>0\), then let \(\eta\downarrow0\). Continuity of \(s\log s\) at zero, of the linear term involving \(\log\sigma\), and of the trace norm gives the stated result. \(\square\)

Why the constant cannot improve

A noncommutative inequality must include commuting examples. Take

\[ \sigma=I/2,\qquad \rho_\varepsilon=\operatorname{diag}(1/2+\varepsilon,1/2-\varepsilon). \]

As \(\varepsilon\to0\), \(D(\rho_\varepsilon\Vert\sigma)=2\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^4)\), while \(\lVert\rho_\varepsilon-\sigma\rVert_1=2|\varepsilon|\). The ratio tends to \(1/2\), so the constant is sharp. With base-two logarithms it becomes \(1/(2\log2)\).

The classical and quantum proofs have the same skeleton: entropy has enough curvature along the line connecting the two states, and a sign test measures the entire discrepancy. Quantum data processing gives another short route via the two-outcome measurement [1]; the direct proof above makes the curvature responsible for the constant explicit. Early quantum forms appear in work of Hiai, Ohya, and Tsukada [4].
